Experimental Design

Key takeaways

  • Most studies focus on syntax, not surprisingly. In many cases, the line between morphology and syntax or syntax and semantics is a bit blurry, but this is not really a main focus, so…
  • Explicit reporting of design features, such as exact numbers of conditions, is not very consistent.
  • Studiues vary widely in the numbers of conditions they include, and many studies, especially earlier ones, report overly complex designs.

Design structure

  • Reporting of condition numbers
  • Conditions
  • Design structure

The exact number of experimental conditions is not consistently reported, although the design can often be reconstructed from the description. Studies often report individual factors and/or design structure (e.g. “We used a 2x3 design crossing factor A with factor B”) but then leave the reader to compute the final total.

In many instances certain contrasts are not considered part of the experimental conditions, thus under-representing the complexity of the design.

How many experimental conditions are there?

There are 344 studies where the total number of conditions is not clear. Those with larger numbers (>10) should probably be re-examined. There are even a few studies (N = 4) with very large (>50) numbers of conditions, which should certain be checked and confirmed.

Again, many of these numbers are deducted / inferred from incomplete descriptions of designs. We’ve included some examples of vague descriptions in Examples of over-complicated design below.

Additional design characteristics

  • Randomization
  • Presentation
  • Other features

Were items presented in a fixed or (semi-)random order across participants?

  • Random: fully random for each participant
  • Semi-random (’psuedo-random): order is largely random, but with some prescribed conditions on order (e.g. no adjacent items of the same condition)
  • Fixed: order is the same across (sets of) participants. Fixed orders are probably more common, as paper questionnaires do not allow for participant-specific re-ordering, but this is not always reported explicitly.

How were the experimental trials organized for presentation?

  • Blocks: items are arranged into and present as predetermined sets
  • Semi-random (’psuedo-random): order is largely random, but with some prescribed conditions on order (e.g. no adjacent items of the same condition)

Here “intermixed” involves items of different conditions being intermixed without blocking. Blocking typically also involves intermixing within blocks.

  • Some studies (N = 113) include pre-test trials, typically as practice trials for the task. This is common with Magnitude Estimation studies. Very few (N = 6) include post-test trials.
  • Many studies balanced items across (predicted) conditions, but this is not always reported clearly.
  • A small percentage of studies involve the same items in multiple tasks.
