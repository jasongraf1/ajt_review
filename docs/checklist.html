A Minimal Checklist for Reporting Experimental Studies in Linguistics

The following checklist sets out key information specifications for reporting experimental Acceptability Judgment Task (AJT) studies in theoretical linguistics. It is designed to encourage clarity and precision without prescribing specific theoretical commitments or statistical frameworks. Our goal is to provide a minimal shared vocabulary for authors, readers and reviewers to draw from (see also our Glossary). This checklist represents a guide for authors to ensure that their work can be understood, evaluated, and reused by fellow scholars, as well as a diagnostic tool for reviewers to notice when key design commitments are underspecified in study reporting. The checklist is also intended to align with open science practices without insisting on preregistration or complex infrastructure.

There are good overviews of how to carefully design AJTs readily available (e.g. Schütze & Sprouse 2013; Goodall 2021; Häussler & Juzek 2021), and much of the information in these recommendations is already commonly reported in modern studies. However, there remains a surprising lack of consistency across authors, journals and publishers in both the terminology used as well as the amount of detail provided when writing up these studies. Each item on this checklist corresponds to a potential confusion/failure point in the reporting of experimental studies, many of which we commonly observed in our review.

1. Research question(s) and aim(s)

Authors should explicitly report:

  • The language or language variety being studied
  • the phenomenon under investigation
  • the specific hypotheses that the study is aiming to test, including which contrasts are hypothesised a priori (predicted comparisons) and which are exploratory

2. Experimental design and conditions

Authors should explicitly report:

  • the dependent variable
  • all manipulated factors (independent variables)
  • the number of levels for each manipulated factor
  • the total number of experimental conditions, i.e. the total number of unique combinations of factor levels
  • whether each factor is within- or between-participants
  • all planned contrasts or comparisons between participant groups (i.e., non-manipulated factors such as age levels, gender, native vs. non-native)

Conditions should be described independently of specific stimuli, so that the full design space is recoverable.

Note: Some continuous factors function as predictors in analysis rather than as design manipulations. These should be described explicitly (e.g., “memory score was included as a continuous predictor”), but they are not part of the experiment’s condition structure unless discretized.

3. Definition of “item”

Authors should explicitly define what counts as a critical item in the study, e.g. a sentence token, a minimally contrasted sentence pair, a lexical root instantiated across frames.

We strongly recommend that the term ‘item’ should be exclusively used to refer to a single unique stimulus that a participant is intended to respond to (see Glossary). If multiple sets of stimulus items or item types are involved (e.g. constructional frames with matched lexical fillers), their relationship should be described.

4. Items and stimulus construction

Authors should explicitly report:

  • the total number of unique items (tokens), including both the number of critical items and filler items, if used
  • how critical items were constructed or selected
  • how filler items were constructed or selected, including proportions of acceptability levels as well as justification for the number of filler items used1
  • whether the same items appear in multiple conditions or whether conditions use disjoint item sets
  • the number of items (tokens) per experimental condition
  • where the complete list of items, including all filler items, can be found

Any controls or constraints on items’ construction or selection (lexical frequency, length, plausibility, animacy, etc.) should be briefly noted.

5. Counterbalancing and presentation

Authors should explicitly report:

  • how items were organised for presentation, including any blocking and/or randomization procedures
  • the number of critical items seen by each participant

If different (counterbalanced) item lists were presented to participants, authors should describe and report the counterbalancing scheme, including:

  • the number of lists and procedures for assigning items and participants to lists
  • how lists were presented to participants

Note: If no counterbalancing is used, i.e. all participants saw all items, this should be stated explicitly.

6. Participants

Authors should explicitly report:

  • the total number of participants initially recruited
  • the population from which participants were sampled, including key inclusion criteria such as region, language background, training in linguistics, age2
  • how participants were recruited (if university students were used, it should be explained how students were notified and brought into the study)
  • post-sampling exclusion criteria and number of exclusions, if any
  • the final number of participants (after any filtering) whose data were included in the analysis
  • how participants were compensated. If no compensation was provided, this should be stated explicitly.
  • how informed consent was obtained from participants

7. Procedure and task

Authors should explicitly describe and report:

  • the task participants performed
  • the exact instructions provided to participants (e.g. as the verbatim instruction text or screen capture in an appendix)
  • the construct the task is intended to tap (acceptability, grammaticality, felicity, plausibility, etc.)
  • the response measure(s) collected (e.g. ratings, choices, reaction times)
  • the exact number of experimental trials each participant saw/heard (this is not the same as the number of critical items)
  • the modality in which the stimuli were delivered (e.g., written, auditory, signed)
  • the means by which participants gave their responses (e.g., computer-based, hand-written, spoken, signed)
  • the setting of the task, including whether it was done under supervision of the researcher (e.g. in a lab or a classroom)
  • whether any timing constraints were placed on performance. If response time was unlimited and/or participants could proceed at their own pace, this should be stated explicitly.
  • whether participants were able to review or change their responses
  • whether participants were able to take breaks during the study
  • any and all software tools used for delivery of the task (e.g. Ibex, Qualtrics)

Any additional aspects of the procedure relevant to inference (e.g. inclusion of practice or calibration trials, inclusion of comprehension questions, pilot-testing) should also be noted.

8. Analysis and inferential structure

Authors should explicitly report:

  • the dependent variable(s)
  • any statistical manipulations to the dependent variable, e.g. log-transform, z-score normalization
  • the statistical model(s) or test(s) used, including the test statistics, measures of effect size3, and where relevant, the threshold of statistical significance (usually α < 0.05)4
  • whether participants and/or items are treated as sources of variability (e.g. by grouping or as random factors). If not done, authors should justify why this is not necessary.
  • how experimental conditions enter the analysis, e.g. as main effects or interactions in a regression model
  • the software tool(s) used for analysis (e.g., R, Python, SPSS)
  • where the data and software code used for the analysis can be obtained

Any additional statistical analyses (e.g., satiation analysis, power analysis or autocorrelation analysis) should be briefly noted and data and code for these should also be made available.

9. Transparency and availability

At minimum, authors should provide:

  • a complete list of all experimental items used in the study, including both critical and filler items (and any check items or comprehension questions)
  • the exact instructions provided to participants
  • the complete dataset and software code used for analysis5
  • indication of whether the study was preregistered, and if so, where
  • where the above can be accessed (or why they cannot be shared)
  • how ethical review was sought, conducted and approved

This information can be included as appendices in the original paper, or better as supplementary materials published with the article or in an online repository. Where to find these materials should be clearly described in a brief data availability statement, or as a link to a public repository.

Footnotes

  1. To our knowledge there is currently no clear consensus on how many fillers should be used, and commonly cited sources give conflicting suggestions for critical-to-filler item ratios ranging from 1:1 (Goodall 2021) to 1:2 or 1:3 (Cowart 1997). This variability is also found in related fields where there is recent evidence that filler items can impact certain kinds of tasks (Arehalli & Wittenberg 2021).↩︎

  2. It’s common to report basic demographic information about participants, but this information is often considered only incidental to the primary research questions, so it is not always reported.↩︎

  3. Test statistics such as \(F\), \(t\), or \(\chi^2\) are not measures of effect size, although regression model coefficients (\(b\) or \(\beta\)) are considered effect size measures. Common effect size measures for ANOVA are (partial) \(\eta^2\) or \(\omega^2\), and Cohen’s \(d\) is often used with t tests.↩︎

  4. The p < 0.05 standard is so ubiquitous that it is almost never mentioned, but we feel it’s good practice to encourage more reflection on data analysis assumptions.↩︎

  5. This is not merely tables of averages or other summary statistics. Authors should provide the (anonymized) complete raw data from the study, including every response from every participant along with all metadata (e.g. demographic information) collected.↩︎
