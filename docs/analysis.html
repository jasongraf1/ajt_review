Analysis

Key takeaways

  • Mixed-effects regression is now the dominant analysis approach, having overtaken ANOVA, which is consistent with the field’s broader shift toward models that can handle by-participant and by-item variability directly rather than aggregating it away. That said, random effect structure is inconsistently reported. Often it is simply reported as “random effects of subject and item”, without specifying the exact random effect structure.
  • Effect sizes are usually absent, except when reported as regression coefficients. Most studies not using regression (e.g. ANOVA or t-tests) report only significance tests and F or t scores, not a standardized measure of effect magnitude such as \(\eta^2\) or Cohen’s \(d\).
  • Power analysis is almost never conducted (or at least almost never reported), despite sample and item sizes varying enormously across the field (see Design and Stimuli).
  • Bayesian methods remain rare, and multiple-comparison corrections are almost never mentioned.
  • Analysis software is reported inconsistently. R is the most common tool when specified, but over half of studies don’t say what software was used at all.

Model specification

  • By-item/subject effects
  • Rating scale treatment
  • Preprocessing

Were adjustments made for by-participant or by-item variation (e.g. as ‘random effects’ or F1 and F2 ANOVA)?

Many studies use separate by-subject and by-item ANOVAs, and we include those here along with mixed-effects regression models. Both approaches are used to simultaneously test whether an effect generalizes across both human participants and stimulus items.

Was the response analysed as a continuous scale, an ordinal factor, or dichotomous value?

This choice matters for which models are appropriate, and doesn’t always match how the scale was presented to participants (see Task). A substantial percentage of studies analyzed Likert-scale ratings as continuous data.

Z-score normalization is by far the most common manipulation, but this is still a minority of studies, although it’s standard in Magnitude Estimation. Log-transformation is also occasionally used.

Reporting rigor: Effect size, power, correction, and thresholds

Effect sizes are usually reported as regression coefficients. Statistical significance (p-value) is almost always reported, but the threshold for “significance” used by the authors is almost never explicitly declared.

Power analysis and multiple comparison correction are very rare.
