Glossary of terms for reporting Acceptability Judgment studies

This is a working glossary of key terms for reporting Acceptability Judgment Task (AJT) studies in theoretical linguistics. The aim is to provide a common vocabulary that authors and reviewers can refer to when reporting/reviewing work involving AJT experiments.

This glossary is by no means exhaustive. We focus on those terms and concepts that are often used in varying ways in the theoretical linguistics literature, with the hope of encouraging clarity and consistency in research reporting.

Experimental Design

  • Judgment: a participant’s evaluation of a linguistic expression. This may take many forms, but most commonly as an indication of an item’s position on a preset scale or as an indication of the relative quality of or preference for an item compared to a common baseline or alternative expression (as in Magnitude Estimation or Forced Choice tasks, respectively). This is the dependent variable.
  • Factor: a property that is systematically varied across items or participants to test a hypothesis, i.e. an independent variable (e.g., island context vs non-island context). Factors may be categorical or continuous.
    • Categorical factor: A factor whose possible values are discrete categories (e.g., island vs non-island, presence vs absence of negation). Each category is a level, and combinations of levels define experimental conditions
    • Continuous factor (covariate): A factor whose values vary along a numeric scale (e.g., memory-test score, lexical frequency, proficiency). Continuous factors do not have levels unless they are explicitly binned for design purposes.1
  • Condition: a unique level, or combination of levels, of a one or more manipulated factors. Each condition corresponds to a systematic difference in some property or combination properties under investigation (e.g., word order, agreement, structural configuration). Conditions are defined independently of individual stimulus items; items instantiate conditions. Conditions usually contrast within-participants but may also contrast between participants if assignment to levels is experimental.
  • Group: a contrast, or combination of contrasts, arising fromfixed participant characteristics (e.g., age group, gender, native vs non-native speaker); groups are levels of factors that are not manipulated by the researcher.
  • Within-participants/subjects: a design in which each participant contributes responses in more than one condition. Differences between conditions are therefore estimated within the same participant.2
  • Between-participants/subjects: a design in which different sets of participants are assigned to different conditions or belong to different groups. Differences are therefore estimated across different sets of participants.
  • Mixed design: a design involving a combination of within- and between-participants comparisons. For example, a study comparing island contexts vs non-island contexts (within-participants) across participant education levels (between-subjects).
  • Counterbalancing: a design strategy in which items, or subsets of items, are distributed across participants in a systematic way. Balancing is often done in such a way that (i) no participant sees an excessive number of highly similar items, (ii) each condition is represented equally across the participants, and (iii) order and fatigue effects are not confounded with any particular condition. In many AJT studies, this is implemented by assigning items from matched sets to different lists, so that each participant sees only a subset of items from each condition, while the full participant sample provides data for all conditions and items.

Items and Trials

  • Item: a unique stimulus token that a participant is intended to respond to.
    • Critical/Target item: an item exhibiting a manipulated feature (or set of features) of specific interest to the researchers. A unique, concrete instantiation of one experimental condition.
    • Filler item: an item NOT of specific interest to the researchers, usually presented as a distractor to mask the purpose of the study.
    • Check item: an item NOT of specific interest to the researcher but included as a check that participants are doing the task as intended, e.g. intermittent comprehension questions. Responses to check items are often used to filter participants post hoc.
  • (Item) Set: two or more items constructed to be comparable across conditions (e.g. involving the same lexical words but differing in structure). Each item in the set instantiates a different experimental condition.
  • (Item) List: a subset of items assigned to one or more participants; common in counterbalanced designs.
  • Trial: a single, unique instance in which a participant must respond to a stimulus.3
    • Pre-test trial: a trial given to participants prior to the test trials; often included to familiarise participants with the task or to help calibrate the instrument (i.e. practice trials); NOT included as part of the data analysis
    • Test trial: a trial forming part of the core data to be analysed; this includes critical items and filler items, which often involve the same task.
    • Check trial: a trial involving response to an attention check item (which often involves a different task from that of the test trials)
    • Post-test trial: a trial given to participants after test trials have been completed; NOT included as part of the data analysis

Task Features and Task Types

  • Task: the specific activity participants are instructed to perform in response to an item (e.g., rate on a scale, choose an option, repeat aloud, paraphrase).
  • Acceptability judgment task (AJT): A task in which participants indicate how natural, acceptable, or well-formed a linguistic expression is, according to their linguistic knowledge or intuitions. Common types include:
    • Rating task (scalar judgements): Participants provide a response on a fixed numerical or ordinal scale
      • Likert-type scale: a rating task using labelled, discrete categories (ordinal scale). Labels may be included for all or only a subset of categories such as the endpoints.
      • Visual analogue scale (VAS): a rating task involving moving a continuous slider or making marks on a fixed line
    • Magnitude Estimation task (ME): a rating task in which participants assign numerical values to items relative to a common reference item (modulus), allowing for proportional judgments rather than fixed-scale ratings (ratio scale).
    • Binary judgment task: a task involving the evaluation of an expression by choosing between two response options (e.g., acceptable vs. unacceptable, yes vs. no, grammatical vs. ungrammatical).
    • Forced-choice task: a task in which participants must identify the better/more acceptable option from two or more alternative expressions presented as a set (ordinal scale).
    • Scalar forced-choice task: a task in which participants must distribute numerical values among options from two or more alternative expressions presented as a set (continuous scale, anchored at both ends). Similar to visual analogue scale ratings, but the scale represents preference for one alternative rather than degree of acceptability.
  • Timed / speeded task: A task in which responses must be made under time constraints (e.g., limited viewing time or response deadline).
  • Self-paced presentation: items are revealed incrementally (e.g. word-by-word, region-by-region), with participants controlling the pace.

Common points of confusion:

Condition–set–item relation:

Conditions are defined by manipulated properties (factor levels). Items instantiate conditions; each item belongs to exactly one condition. Items may be constructed as matching sets in which different conditions are instantiated using “the same” (or similar) forms. For example, the following two sets contain items instantiating six conditions crossing 3 structures (Sentential subject, Extraposition, Island) with 2 polarity (positive/negative) values.

  1. Set 1
    1. That she knew Bob bothered John. [Sentential subject, positive]
    2. It bothered John that she knew Bob. [Extraposition, positive]
    3. Who did that she knew bother John? [Island, positive]
    4. That she knew Bob didn’t bother John. [Sentential subject, negative]
    5. It didn’t bother John that she knew Bob. [Extraposition, negative]
    6. Who did that she knew not bother John? [Island, negative]
  2. Set 2
    1. That Mary bought a new chainsaw surprised Alex. [Sentential subject, positive]
    2. It surprised Alex that Mary bought a new chainsaw. [Extraposition, positive]
    3. What did that Mary bought surprise Alex? [Island, positive]
    4. That Mary bought a new chainsaw didn’t surprise Alex. [Sentential subject, negative]
    5. It didn’t surprise Alex that Mary bought a new chainsaw. [Extraposition, negative]
    6. What did that Mary bought not surprise Alex? [Island, negative]

When a condition is represented by multiple unique instantiations, these are treated as distinct items, which may be distributed across participants using counterbalancing. In the above case there are six conditions each represented by two items, for a total of 12 items altogether.

A model description of items and procedures, where exact numbers and design features are explicitly reported might look like so:

“We created 12 sets of stimulus items where each set consisted of 6 items instantiating one of the six experimental conditions. This resulted in a total of 72 unique critical items, with 12 items per condition. These items were distributed across six counterbalanced lists (12 sentences per list) such that only one item from each set was included in a given list. The critical items in each list were intermixed amongst an additional set of 24 filler items, and all participants saw the same 24 fillers. Fillers were constructed such that […] Each participant saw only one list, and item order was randomized for each participant. Each participant thus responded to a total of 36 (12 critical + 24 filler) judgment trials.

The experiment was conducted via Qualtrics, and a URL link to the experiment was provided through the Prolific platform. Participants thus completed the experiment remotely in various settings without supervision of the researchers. Participants first saw a landing page with a description of the study and a consent form (Appendix A.1). Upon registering their affirmative consent, participants moved to the instruction page where the judgment task was described (Appendix A.2). Participants were asked to rate the acceptability of each sentence on a 7-point scale from 1 (totally unacceptable) to 7 (totally acceptable). We presented them with 3 example items illustrating a hypothetical 1, 4, and 7 rating.

For presentation, all items were shown in writing on an otherwise blank page, with one item per page without additional context and with no time limit. Participants could proceed at their own pace through the experiment, pausing or taking breaks as needed. Participants could not return to previous items and change their responses after they had entered them. Response times were recorded but not used for analysis. The average total time for completion was 15 minutes.”

Footnotes

  1. Continuous factors may influence responses and may be included in the analysis, but they do not by themselves constitute distinct experimental conditions unless the values are discretized by design (e.g., “high memory vs low memory” groups).↩︎

  2. Participants do not necessarily see every item in every condition; they may see a subset of items per condition in a counterbalanced list, but their responses still span multiple conditions.↩︎

  3. ‘Item’ and ‘trial’ are not equivalent. For instance, a study may involve 10 items (unique sentences) each presented to participants for judgment three separate times, for a total of (10 x 3) = 30 trials. Likewise, a single participant may only see a subset of the total items used across a complete study.↩︎
