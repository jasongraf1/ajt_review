Per-subject z-scoring and mixed-effects models for acceptability judgments

1. Z-scoring and the by-subject intercept

  • Per-subject z-scoring sets each participant’s mean to 0 and SD to 1 over the trials used for normalisation.
  • If those are exactly the trials being modelled, and the design is balanced (Latin square, no exclusions), every subject mean is 0. The by-subject intercept variance is then estimated at or near zero, and lme4 typically reports a singular fit. Dropping the term is harmless in that case.
  • This guarantee breaks when:
    • z-scores are computed over all items including fillers (as recommended by Schütze & Sprouse 2014), so critical-item means are no longer 0;
    • the data are unbalanced (exclusions, missing trials, unequal lists).
  • Quick check: compute each subject’s mean z-score over the modelled data. Non-trivial variation means the intercept should stay.

2. Random slopes still matter

  • Z-scoring removes between-subject differences in location and spread, not differences in the size of condition effects.
  • The argument of Barr et al. (2013) still applies: omitting by-subject slopes for within-subject factors inflates Type I error. By-item effects are unaffected by subject-level normalisation.
  • Recommendation: specify the design-justified (maximal) structure. In lme4, prune a zero-variance intercept if it causes a singular fit and report it (cf. Matuschek et al. 2017). In brms, keep it; a regularising prior shrinks it towards zero.

3. Between-subjects factors (e.g. L1 vs L2, age group)

  • Group main effect. Normalising over the modelled trials makes every subject’s mean 0, so the group main effect is removed by construction. Normalising over fillers too leaves a relative group effect (critical items relative to each person’s overall rating distribution), which must be interpreted as such.
  • Group × condition interactions survive but can be distorted, because dividing by subject SD conflates response style with sensitivity:
    • participants who discriminate sharply have larger SDs because of their large contrasts, so their effects shrink;
    • groups that compress their responses (mid-scale, cautious raters) have their effects inflated.
  • Interactions on a rating scale are scale-dependent: monotonic transformations can create, remove or reverse them (Loftus 1978; Wagenmakers et al. 2012).
  • The underlying problem is general. Separating “this group finds X less acceptable” from “this group uses the scale differently” is underdetermined from ratings alone. Z-scoring resolves it by assumption. Ordinal models face the same identifiability issue (group-specific thresholds are confounded with a group location effect) but make the assumption explicit.
  • Anchors break the ambiguity: fillers of known status or standard reference items (Featherston 2021) that both groups should treat alike. Normalise against, or contrast with, those items.
  • Random effects: no by-subject slope for group, but items seen by both groups need by-item slopes for group (and group × condition).

4. Analysis recommendations

  1. Prefer ordinal (cumulative) mixed models for Likert-type ratings (Bürkner & Vuorre 2019; Liddell & Kruschke 2018). Model scale-use differences in the model, e.g. by-subject variation in thresholds or disc in brms.
  2. For between-subjects factors, decide explicitly whether thresholds/disc vary by group, and justify that with anchor items.
  3. Treat z-scored Gaussian models as a robustness check, and state that the group main effect is not interpretable under per-subject normalisation.
  4. If a key interaction holds on one scale but not another, report it rather than resolving it silently.
  5. Normalisation estimates each subject’s mean and SD from the same data before modelling, so model SEs ignore that uncertainty. This is usually negligible but favours doing it within the model.

5. Possible coding feature for the review

Whether studies with between-subjects factors normalised ratings before testing group effects, and whether they acknowledge what the normalisation removes.
